LU1 · Cost Concepts

Cost classification & terminology · fixed vs variable · direct vs indirect · high/low method

Direct costs

Costs that can be traced easily to a cost object.
  • Direct materials: raw materials transformed into the finished product; integral part of it. Delivery cost and import duties on purchase are usually included.
  • Direct labour: labour directly converting materials into finished products (e.g. wages of bricklayers).

Manufacturing overheads

Manufacturing costs that cannot easily be traced to specific units produced, e.g. indirect materials and indirect labour.

Cost behaviour

How a cost reacts to a change in volume/activity.
  • Fixed: total does not change with production within a range (rent, salaries, insurance, depreciation); incurred even at zero output.
  • Variable: total changes in direct proportion; per unit stays the same.
  • Mixed (semi-variable): fixed + variable part, e.g. electricity: fixed monthly charge + rate per kWh.

Separating mixed costs

High/low method · scatter graph · simple regression. High/low is simple but not the most accurate (only two observations). Since fixed cost doesn't change, the change in cost between levels is variable cost.

LU2 · Material Cost

Cost of inventory · journals · perpetual vs periodic · FIFO & weighted average

Inventory flow

Raw materials: opening + purchases − issued to production = closing.
WIP: opening + raw materials issued + direct labour + manufacturing overheads − completed = closing.
Finished goods: opening + completed − sold = closing.

Perpetual vs periodic

Perpetual: cost of sale recorded every time a sale is made; inventory account updated.
Periodic: purchases account updated on each purchase; physical stock count at period end. COGS = opening + purchases − closing.

FIFO

Items bought first are used/sold/issued first, so stock on hand at year-end is the latest inventory received.

Weighted average (WAM)

Every new purchase is added to stock and a new average is calculated (total cost on hand ÷ units on hand); that average is used for cost of sales.

LU3 · Labour Cost

Remuneration · overtime · deductions · labour recovery rate

Terminology

Direct vs indirect labour · normal time · overtime · gross pay · deductions · Net pay = Gross − Deductions

Remuneration methods

Fixed salary: regardless of quantity/time.
Hourly wages: (normal + overtime hours) × rate per hour.

Deductions

Legal deductions: PAYE and UIF. PAYE is on taxable income = gross + taxable fringe benefits − employee's pension contribution.

Productive hours

Total available hours − annual leave − public holidays − (sick leave) − idle time = productive hours. Idle time = when production stops and direct labour is unoccupied.
Example 1 · weekly net salary

Normal week 45 h (Mon–Fri 8 h, Sat 5 h). Worked: Mon 8, Tue 8, Wed 10, Thu 8, Fri 8, Sat 5, Sun 3 = 50 h. Rate R200/h; overtime 1.5×, Sunday 2×. Employee pension 7.5% of normal pay; employee medical R720; PAYE 13% of taxable income; UIF R200.

Hours: normal 45 · overtime 2 (Wednesday) · Sunday 3 Normal pay 45 × R200 R9 000 Overtime 2 × R200 × 1.5 R600 Sunday 3 × R200 × 2 R1 200 Gross R10 800 Less pension 7.5% × R9 000 (R675) ← on NORMAL pay Taxable income R10 125 Less: PAYE 13% × R10 125 R1 316.25 + medical R720 + UIF R200 = (R2 236.25) Net salary R7 888.75

Employer contributions (5% pension, R720 medical) are not deducted from the employee; they are a cost to the employer.

Example 2 · labour recovery rate per hour

52 weeks; 3 weeks leave; 6 public holidays; idle time 15%; sick leave 5 days; 40 h/week; annual pay R84 000; employer pension 6%, medical 8%; bonus = 1/12 of annual pay.

STEP 1 Cost to employer Normal pay R84 000 + bonus (÷12) R7 000 + pension 6% R5 040 + medical 8% R6 720 = R102 760 STEP 2 Productive hours 52 − 3 = 49 weeks × 40 = 1 960 h − public holidays 6 × 8 = (48) − sick leave 5 × 8 = (40) = 1 872 h − idle time 15% × 1 872 = (280.8) = 1 591.2 productive hours STEP 3 Rate = R102 760 ÷ 1 591.2 = R64.58 per hour Product "G" takes 3 h → 3 × R64.58 = R193.74 direct labour

Idle time is a % of hours paid to be at work (after leave, holidays and sick leave), not of the 1 960.

LU4 · Manufacturing Overheads

Classification · primary & secondary allocation · departmental recovery rates · overhead per product

Examples of manufacturing overheads

Supervisor salaries, factory rent, machine depreciation, electricity, indirect materials (glue, thread, cleaning agents), equipment maintenance, factory insurance, security, factory supplies, canteen & sanitation, waste disposal, indirect labour (cleaners, helpers).

Departmentalisation

Primary allocation: initial allocation of overheads to all departments (production and service).
Secondary allocation: service department overheads reallocated to the production departments.

Cost drivers (Drips example)

Supervisor salaries → number of employees
Rent → floor space
Depreciation → value of machinery
Electricity → kilowatt hours
In an assessment the driver will be given; it can differ between companies.

Also in the LU4 criteria (no worked example in your slides)

Classify OH as fixed, variable, semi-fixed or semi-variable; separate OH into fixed/variable (use high/low from LU1); budgeted vs applied vs actual; under/over-applied overheads; OH journal entries.
